# discriminantly
## Constitution v0.2

### Preamble

Discriminantly exists to preserve, express, and make useful a person's taste.

Taste is not a static profile and it is not reducible to a list of preferences. It emerges from what a person notices, remembers, experiences, recommends, collects, and stands behind.

Discriminantly therefore records not only **what** a person finds interesting, but **their relationship to it**.

The system should make those relationships useful to the person, to the people they choose to share them with, and—with the person's permission—to the artificial intelligences that act on their behalf.

The fundamental principle is:

> **The user's taste belongs to the user. Discriminantly exists to preserve it, not to define it.**

---

# I. The Objects of Discriminantly

Discriminantly currently recognizes two primary objects: **Notes** and **Travel Marks**.

## 1. Notes

A **Note** is a record of a physical object that the user wishes to remember.

A Note may represent an object that caught the user's attention, an object they own, an object they encountered, or an object they may wish to acquire.

A Note does not inherently mean:

> “I like this.”

It means:

> **“This object is worth remembering to me.”**

The distinction is intentional.

---

## 2. Travel Marks

A **Travel Mark** is a record of a place that the user wishes to remember.

A Travel Mark may represent a place the user:

- wishes to visit;
- has visited;
- recommends;
- encountered;
- or otherwise considers worthy of remembering.

A Travel Mark does not inherently establish that the user likes the place.

---

## 3. Check-ins

A **Check-in** explicitly records that a user visited a Travel Mark.

A Check-in therefore carries a different kind of evidence from a Mark.

A Mark says:

> “This place matters enough for me to record.”

A Check-in says:

> **“I have actually been there.”**

A Check-in should therefore be treated as evidence of experience, not automatically as evidence of approval.

---

## 4. Collections

A **Collection** is a deliberate grouping of Notes and/or Travel Marks.

Collections represent a user's act of curation.

A Collection should not automatically be interpreted as a statement that every object within it is equally liked or endorsed.

However, the act of grouping objects together may itself provide useful contextual evidence about how the user thinks about them.

Collections therefore represent **contextual taste**, rather than necessarily explicit preference.

---

# II. The Warrant

## 5. Warrant

A **Warrant** is an explicit act of endorsement.

The concept takes its inspiration from the traditional idea of a royal warrant: an indication that someone is willing to stand behind something.

A Warrant means:

> **“I stand behind this.”**

It is stronger than merely noticing, recording, collecting, or experiencing something.

A Warrant should therefore be treated as a high-value taste signal.

A Warrant does not necessarily mean:

> “This is my favorite.”

Nor does it mean:

> “I will always like this.”

It means that, at the time of warranting, the user was willing to put their judgment behind it.

The action of Warranting should be deliberately lightweight.

It should feel closer to starring something than writing a review.

---

# III. Taste and Evidence

## 6. Not all signals are equal

Discriminantly recognizes that different actions provide different kinds of evidence.

For example:

**Note**

> “I noticed this and want to remember it.”

**Mark**

> “This place is worth remembering.”

**Check-in**

> “I experienced this place.”

**Collection**

> “I have deliberately grouped this with these other things.”

**Warrant**

> “I stand behind this.”

No single action should be assumed to completely describe the user's opinion.

---

## 7. Explicit versus inferred taste

Discriminantly should prefer explicit signals over inferred ones.

An AI may infer patterns from a user's record, but it must distinguish:

- what the user explicitly stated;
- what the user explicitly did;
- what the system can observe;
- and what the AI merely believes follows from those observations.

Inference is useful.

Inference is not fact.

---

# IV. Taste Through the Network

## 8. Taste can be observed relationally

A person's taste may be expressed not only through the things they create, but through the things they repeatedly choose from other people's records.

For example, if a user repeatedly re-notes objects originally recorded by another user, that behavior may indicate an overlap in taste.

This is different from an explicit statement:

> “I have the same taste as this person.”

It is evidence from behavior.

---

## 9. Re-noting as a taste signal

When a user re-notes a Note or Mark originally recorded by another user, discriminantly should preserve that relationship.

The system should be able to distinguish:

> “Brian noted this.”

from:

> **“Brian noted this after Alice had noted it.”**

Repeated overlap may provide evidence that the two users share an affinity in a particular area.

The signal becomes stronger through repeated, meaningful overlap.

However:

> **Following someone is not sufficient evidence of shared taste.**

Nor is a single overlapping Note or Mark sufficient to establish meaningful similarity.

---

## 10. Taste affinity

Discriminantly may infer **taste affinity** between users based on meaningful patterns of overlap.

Taste affinity is an inference, not an explicit user declaration.

It should preferably be specific rather than absolute.

For example:

> “Brian and Alice appear to have overlapping taste in hotels.”

is preferable to:

> “Brian and Alice have the same taste.”

A user may have strong affinity with another person in one domain and little or none in another.

Taste affinity may therefore be contextual, directional, and subject to change.

---

## 11. The network should amplify discernment, not popularity

Discriminantly's network should not treat popularity as a substitute for taste.

The most valuable connection is not necessarily:

> “Everyone likes this.”

It may instead be:

> **“Someone whose taste repeatedly overlaps with yours has found this.”**

The system should favor meaningful taste relationships over raw engagement or popularity wherever practical.

---

# V. Privacy and Ownership

## 12. The user owns their taste

A user's discriminantly record represents their personal taste.

The user controls who may access it.

---

## 13. Public and Private

Notes and Travel Marks may be **Public** or **Private**.

This distinction primarily governs **human/social visibility**.

Private does not inherently mean:

> “This information must never be available to AI.”

A user may authorize an AI to access private Notes and Travel Marks for the purpose of personalizing its interactions with that user.

Such authorization does not make the underlying information public.

In other words:

> **Private means private from people and audiences the user has not authorized—not necessarily private from an AI the user has explicitly authorized.**

---

## 14. AI access requires permission

An AI must not access a user's private discriminantly information unless the user has explicitly granted permission for that access.

Permission should be understandable to the user and should not be hidden inside unrelated terms.

The user should be able to revoke that permission.

---

# VI. AI and Discriminantly

## 15. AI is a custodian, not the owner

An AI connected to discriminantly acts on behalf of the user.

It may help the user discover, interpret, organize, and maintain their taste record.

But:

> **The AI does not own the record, and its interpretation is not the user's identity.**

---

## 16. Four levels of AI behavior

AI interaction with discriminantly should be understood as four increasingly consequential activities.

### Observe

The AI reads the user's record.

It may use that information to improve a response.

No change is made to the user's record.

### Infer

The AI identifies a possible preference or pattern.

For example:

> “You seem to consistently prefer smaller independent hotels.”

An inference is not automatically written into the user's record.

### Suggest

The AI proposes an action.

For example:

> “You seem very enthusiastic about this. Would you like me to Warrant it?”

The user may accept or reject the suggestion.

### Act

The AI changes the user's discriminantly record.

Acting requires appropriate authorization.

---

# VII. AI-Created Warrants

## 17. AI may eventually Warrant on behalf of the user

An AI may be authorized to create Warrants on the user's behalf.

This is potentially one of the most important capabilities of the system.

A user may choose to grant:

- no Warrant authority;
- confirmation-required Warrant authority;
- or automatic Warrant authority under defined conditions.

---

## 18. Automatic Warranting must require meaningful evidence

An AI must not Warrant an object merely because:

- the user searched for it;
- the user viewed it;
- the user mentioned it;
- the user Marked it;
- the user placed it in a Collection;
- the AI itself recommended it;
- or the user asked for information about it.

These actions establish interest or context, not endorsement.

Conversational evidence may justify a Warrant when the user's language or behavior demonstrates a sufficiently strong positive judgment.

For example:

> “That was one of the best meals I've had.”

is materially different from:

> “Tell me about this restaurant.”

The first is evidence of endorsement.

The second is merely curiosity.

---

## 19. Confirmation mode

When Warrant authorization is confirmation-based, the AI must request confirmation before creating a Warrant.

Example:

> “You sound like you really stand behind this. Shall I Warrant it?”

The AI must not create the Warrant until confirmation is received.

---

## 20. Automatic mode

When the user has explicitly authorized automatic warranting, the AI may create Warrants without requesting confirmation for each individual action.

However, the AI must still:

1. apply the defined evidence threshold;
2. preserve provenance;
3. avoid warranting based solely on weak signals;
4. remain within the user's authorization;
5. permit subsequent correction or revocation.

---

# VIII. Provenance

## 21. Taste signals should retain their origin

Whenever practical, discriminantly should preserve enough information to distinguish:

- something the user created directly;
- something the user did after seeing another user's record;
- something generated through conversation with an AI;
- and something inferred by the system.

The provenance of a signal is part of its meaning.

A user's taste record should not become an opaque collection of conclusions whose origins can no longer be understood.

---

# IX. Corrections and Reversibility

## 22. Taste can change

A user's taste is not immutable.

A Warrant created today does not permanently establish a preference.

Users must be able to revoke or modify their own records.

An AI should not interpret a previous Warrant as proof that the user will always endorse something.

---

## 23. AI mistakes are not user preferences

An AI-generated action that the user rejects should not subsequently be treated as evidence of the user's taste.

If an AI incorrectly Warrants something, the correction should remove or invalidate the erroneous signal rather than allowing the mistake to become recursive evidence.

---

# X. The Social Graph

## 24. Following and Followers

Following and Followers represent relationships between people.

They may provide useful contextual information about whose taste a user chooses to observe.

However:

> **Following someone does not mean agreeing with them.**

Likewise, having followers does not imply endorsement of those followers' tastes.

Social relationships should therefore remain distinct from explicit taste signals.

---

# XI. Network Discovery

## 25. People can become taste sources

Discriminantly may use taste affinity to help users discover people whose records may be particularly relevant to them.

The objective is not to identify the most popular person.

It is to identify people whose **recorded judgment repeatedly proves useful to this particular user**.

A user's behavior may therefore create a personalized network of trusted taste sources without requiring the user to explicitly construct that network.

---

## 26. Taste affinity should remain an inference

The system should be cautious about labeling people as having similar taste.

Repeated overlap provides evidence.

It does not establish identity of taste.

Where appropriate, discriminantly should communicate affinity in probabilistic or contextual terms rather than presenting it as an objective fact.

---

# XII. The Principle of Minimum Inference

Discriminantly should capture the richest evidence available without pretending to know more than the evidence supports.

The system should prefer:

> **“Brian warranted this restaurant.”**

over:

> **“Brian likes restaurants like this.”**

The second statement may eventually be useful as an inference.

But the first is an observed fact.

Likewise, the system should prefer:

> **“Brian repeatedly re-notes objects recorded by Alice.”**

over:

> **“Brian and Alice have the same taste.”**

The second may be a useful inference, but the first is the evidence from which it is derived.

---

# XIII. The Purpose of the MCP Layer

The MCP interface exists to make the user's taste portable.

A user should not have to repeatedly teach every AI system:

> what they like,  
> what they have experienced,  
> what they stand behind,  
> and what they are interested in.

With permission, an AI should be able to consult discriminantly as an external, persistent record of the user's taste.

With appropriate permission, it should also be able to contribute to that record.

The ultimate goal is therefore not merely:

> **“AI can read discriminantly.”**

It is:

> **“A user's taste can travel with them.”**

---

# XIV. The Governing Principle

The system should always distinguish among:

> **What the user noticed.**  
> **What the user experienced.**  
> **What the user explicitly endorsed.**  
> **What the user repeatedly chose from others.**  
> **What the network suggests.**  
> **What the AI inferred.**

These are different things.

Discriminantly should never collapse them into one.

---

# XV. Final Principle

Discriminantly should make it effortless for a person to preserve their taste.

It should make that taste useful to the people and systems they choose to share it with.

It should allow useful taste relationships to emerge naturally from the network without reducing those relationships to popularity.

And when an AI is entrusted to act on their behalf, it should become a careful custodian of that record—not an author of a fictionalized version of the person.

> **The human creates the taste.  
> The network reveals affinities.  
> Discriminantly preserves the record.  
> AI helps carry it forward.**
